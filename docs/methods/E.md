## 3. From raw data to the test sample
1. **Announcements.** One per company and day among tracker companies matched to an SEC filer: 905 from 350 companies (`announcements` in `analysis/hypotheses2_a.py`, line 28).
2. **Period.** 2020-03-11 (start of the tracker) to 2026-06-30: 864.
3. **Earnings releases.** For each company, the filing dates of 8-K filings that list item 2.02 ("Results of Operations and Financial Condition"), the filing that accompanies an earnings release (`earnings_dates` in `analysis/hypotheses2_a.py`, line 132): 336 companies, 9,225 releases. Only the recent filings list in each submissions file is used.
4. **Known calendar.** An announcement is kept only if its company has at least 3 releases in that calendar year (so the release calendar is known that year; for large filers the recent list does not reach back to 2020-2021): **708 announcements from 306 companies** (`event_table` in `analysis/hypotheses2_a.py`, line 158). By year: 2020: 22, 2021: 6, 2022: 146, 2023: 242, 2024: 135, 2025: 99, 2026: 58.
5. **Business days.** Weekend announcement dates move to the next Monday (`_bday` in `analysis/hypotheses2_a.py`, line 138); windows count Monday-Friday only (public holidays are not removed).
6. **Window.** "Near" = within 3 business days before or after a release, i.e. a 7-business-day window (`window_days` in `analysis/hypotheses2_a.py`, line 142).
7. **Expected chance.** For each announcement, p_i = share of the year's business days that lie in that company's windows that year (on average 11.2 %). Expected count E = sum of p_i = 79.1.

## 4. The test
- **Primary.** Observed count O vs. the distribution of the count when every announcement moves to a random business day of its own year; then each announcement is near a release with probability p_i, so the null count is simulated as a sum of independent draws, 10,000 times. Two-sided p = 2 x min(P(S >= O), P(S <= O)), with the +1 correction; the smallest value 10,000 simulations can show is 0.0002. Effect: O / E with a 95 % bootstrap interval resampling companies (5,000 times).
- **Why.** Each announcement is compared with its own company's calendar in its own year, so companies with many or few releases and busy or quiet years each get their own expected rate; the simulation needs no distributional assumption.
- **Secondary.** Days 0 to +3 only: 174 vs 45.3 expected (3.84x); days -3 to -1 only: 48 vs 34.0 (1.41x, p = 0.023); window of 1 business day: 5.26x; window of 5: 2.00x; 2022-2023 only: 2.74x.
- **Placebo.** All release dates shifted by +45, -45, +30, -30 calendar days (between real releases): ratios 0.82, 0.92, 0.79, 1.14; p = 0.088, 0.505, 0.054, 0.198: no clustering away from real releases.
- **Power (computed before the comparison).** Detectable excess about 23 announcements (ratio 1.30).
- **Multiple testing.** Round 2 family; at most 4 x 0.0002 = 0.0008 after Holm, so the verdict does not depend on A2 and F.

```python
def test_e(t: pd.DataFrame, n_sim: int = 10000, n_boot: int = 5000, seed: int = 0) -> dict:
    """Observed count near a release vs. the count when every announcement moves to a random business day of its own year
    (each is then near with probability p_i). Two-sided simulation p; O / E with a company bootstrap interval."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    p, near = t["p"].to_numpy(float), t["near"].to_numpy(int)
    O, E = int(near.sum()), float(p.sum())
    sims = (rng.random((n_sim, len(p))) < p).sum(axis=1)
    pval = float(min(1.0, 2 * min((np.sum(sims >= O) + 1) / (n_sim + 1), (np.sum(sims <= O) + 1) / (n_sim + 1))))
    groups = {c: g.index.to_numpy() for c, g in t.groupby("cik")}
    keys = np.array(list(groups))
    ratios = []
    for _ in range(n_boot):
        rows = np.concatenate([groups[c] for c in rng.choice(keys, len(keys))])
        ratios.append(near[rows].sum() / p[rows].sum())
    sd = float(np.sqrt((p * (1 - p)).sum()))
    return {"n": len(t), "n_companies": int(t["cik"].nunique()), "observed": O, "expected": E, "ratio": O / E if E else None,
            "ci_lo": float(np.percentile(ratios, 2.5)), "ci_hi": float(np.percentile(ratios, 97.5)), "p": pval, "predicted_sign": O > E,
            "mean_window_share": float(p.mean()), "mde_excess": 2.8 * sd, "mde_ratio": (E + 2.8 * sd) / E if E else None}
```

## 7. How to read it, and its limits
- *Skoraj tretjina napovedi odpuščanj (219 od 708) je prišla v treh delovnih dneh okoli objave četrtletnih rezultatov podjetja, čeprav bi jih po
  naključju pričakovali le okoli 79. Večina jih pride takoj po objavi: podjetja odpuščanja pogosto napovejo kar skupaj z rezultati.*
- Limits: the tracker date is the date of the news report, which can lag the company's own statement by a day or two (the window covers this); 8-K item 2.02 marks the filing of results, not the conference call; clustering shows timing, not why companies choose it; companies without a known calendar that year are left out.

## 8. Likely questions and answers
- **Isn't this obvious?** It is plausible, but now it is measured: 2.8 times the chance rate, and only around real release dates (the shifted-date placebo finds nothing).
- **Why business days?** Announcements and releases happen on working days; counting weekends would make the expected rate wrong.
- **Why the 8-K item 2.02 and not the 10-Q date?** The 8-K with item 2.02 is filed on the day results are released; 10-Qs are filed days or weeks later.
- **Could the news date be wrong?** It can lag by a day or two; the 3-business-day window absorbs that, and the 1-day window shows an even stronger effect (5.3x).
- **Why are 2020-2021 thin?** For large filers the SEC's recent-filings list does not reach back that far, so those company-years have no known calendar and are left out by the registered rule.
