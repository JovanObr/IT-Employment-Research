## 1. Question and registration
- **Question.** Did US tech companies that announced a layoff in 2022-2023 lose more employees from end-2021 to end-2023 than tech companies that did not?
- **Prediction.** Negative difference (announcers shrink more).
- **Registration.** `docs/hypotheses/PREREGISTRATION.md`, section C, commit `1de58cb 2026-11-14 09:05:20 +0100` (before any test was run). Amendments: none.
- **Known before registering.** Notebook 09 had shown that 2022-2023 headcount change is unrelated to 2019-2021 hiring growth; headcount by layoff status had not been looked at.
- **Why this replaced the planned C.** The planned semiconductor vs. software test had 60 % power (only 10 semiconductor firms with variation) and was dropped before testing.

## 3. From raw data to the test sample
1. **Headcounts.** `collectors/sec_headcount.py` lists, for every tech company (SIC 357x, 366x, 367x, 737x with at least 8 revenue quarters: 995 companies),
   its 10-K filings from the SEC submissions file (`annual_filings` in `collectors/sec_headcount.py`, line 135), streams the main document and reads the first *dated* headcount sentence
   ("As of December 31, 2021, we had approximately 2,447 full-time employees") with `find_headcount` in `collectors/sec_headcount.py`, line 103. Hand checks of random sentences found about
   9 in 10 correct; three error types were fixed (undated numbers from tables or biographies, subsets such as "employees engaged in R&D", part-time counts),
   and `recheck` (`recheck` in `collectors/sec_headcount.py`, line 225) applies the final rules to every saved sentence.
2. **Period year.** A fiscal year ending January-March counts as the previous calendar year (`period_year` in `collectors/sec_headcount.py`, line 130), so the headcount refers to roughly the end of that calendar year.
3. **Companies with both years.** 428 tech companies have a headcount for 2021 and 2023.
4. **Parsing errors.** Changes larger than a factor of 10 are treated as parsing errors and dropped: 5 companies. **Final sample: 423 companies.**
5. **Exposure.** A company is "announced" if a tracker announcement dated 2022-01-01 to 2023-12-31 belongs to a tracker company matched to its CIK (`announced_ciks` in `analysis/hypotheses.py`, line 60): **102 announced, 321 not.**
6. **Outcome.** log(employees 2023 / employees 2021) (`headcount_sample` in `analysis/hypotheses.py`, line 66).
7. **Size (secondary only).** Last 2021 value of log total assets in the company panel; available for 414 companies.

## 4. The test
- **Primary.** Mann-Whitney U, two-sided, alpha 0.05. Effect: median(announced) - median(not), 95 % interval from 5,000 bootstrap resamples of companies.
- **Why.** Headcount changes have heavy tails (acquisitions can double a company; a few parsing errors survive), so a test on ranks is not pulled around by
  a handful of extreme firms; a t-test on means would be. Mann-Whitney gives no effect size, so the median difference with a bootstrap interval is reported beside it.
- **Assumptions.** Independent companies (one row each); no distributional assumption.
- **Secondary.** OLS of the outcome on announced + log assets + SIC 3-digit dummies, HC3 standard errors (big firms announce more and may change differently);
  the same comparison for 2022 -> 2023.
- **Placebo.** The announced label shuffled across companies 1,000 times: false-positive rate **4.5 %** (expected about 5 %).
- **Power.** Minimum detectable shift 0.123 log points (about 12 %) at 80 % power.
- **Multiple testing.** Round 1 family A, B, C, D; Holm-adjusted p = **0.579**.

```python
def test_c(d: pd.DataFrame, n_boot: int = 5000, seed: int = 0) -> dict:
    """Primary: Mann-Whitney U (two-sided); effect = median(announced) - median(not), 95 % bootstrap interval over companies."""
    a, b = d.loc[d["announced"] == 1, "outcome"].to_numpy(), d.loc[d["announced"] == 0, "outcome"].to_numpy()
    u = stats.mannwhitneyu(a, b, alternative="two-sided")
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    boots = [np.median(rng.choice(a, len(a))) - np.median(rng.choice(b, len(b))) for _ in range(n_boot)]
    diff = float(np.median(a) - np.median(b))
    return {"n_announced": len(a), "n_not": len(b), "median_announced": float(np.median(a)), "median_not": float(np.median(b)),
            "diff_median": diff, "ci_lo": float(np.percentile(boots, 2.5)), "ci_hi": float(np.percentile(boots, 97.5)),
            "p": float(u.pvalue), "predicted_sign": diff < 0, "mde_log": mde(float(d["outcome"].std()), len(a), len(b))}
```

## 7. How to read it, and its limits
- *V oknu 2021-2023, ki smo ga izbrali vnaprej, se podjetja z napovedjo odpuščanj niso zanesljivo razlikovala od ostalih. Ko pogledamo le leti 2022-2023,
  pa je razlika jasna: zaposlovanje v letu 2022 je verjetno prikrilo kasnejše krčenje. To smo kasneje potrdili na novih podatkih (hipoteza C2).*
- Limits: headcount is one day a year and read from text; announcements can concern staff abroad; acquisitions change headcount; a company can cut and hire in the same year.

## 8. Likely questions and answers
- **Why not a t-test?** The outcome has heavy tails; a few acquisitions would decide a mean. Mann-Whitney uses ranks.
- **Why 2021-2023 and not 2022-2023?** It was fixed in advance to include all of the 2022 announcements' effect. Switching after seeing results would be p-hacking; the 2022-2023 result was therefore only exploratory, and C2 tested it properly on new years.
- **How reliable are the parsed headcounts?** About 9 in 10 correct in hand checks; three error types fixed; the rules have tests built from the real errors; changes above 10x are dropped.
- **Are non-announcers really layoff-free?** No; the tracker misses some layoffs, which would make the groups more alike and the test conservative.
- **Is it normalised for company size?** The outcome is a relative change (log ratio), so a 10 % cut counts the same at 100 or 100,000 employees; size enters the secondary regression.
